It's hard to overestimate the role symmetry plays in physics. It's also hard to overestimate the
lack of originality that lies in writing a blog post about symmetry. But here we are and like every
other physics student that has wandered off into machine learning I will keep my physics idols from
turning in their graves by mentioning symmetry in my work here and there. Telling everyone how important it
also is for a neural neutwork to respect symmetry (until someone with more compute comes along to show that
it can do without; but lets not tell anyone about this (bitter lesson) [http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html])

Is symmetry general enough a method to not hinder a model from learning?
How can we stop building in our theories of mind?

Now, what do we mean by symmetry? When I hear the word symmetry, I either imagine the face of a (almost objectively)
good looking person or a butterfly. They have in common that, if we were to draw a line along their longitudinal axis exactly in the middle
of the transversal axis (say at $x=0$), it does not matter whether we move left ($x < 0$) or right ($x > 0$) from the axis - it looks the same.
That is, if we imagine a tiny ant with rather bad short term memory being positioned exactly at this line that starts to walk off into
some direction that is not completely parallel to the long axis ($x \neq 0$) after a while (this "while" depending on the short term memory
of the ant), the ant could not tell whether it has walked off left or right. Surely, it can tell whether it walked downwards or upwards as, generally,
the face above our nose does look different than our face below. So from the perspective of the ant,
it makes no difference whether it walks to the left or the right. Its surrounding will be the same.
Well, this is not exactly true for the bespoke reason of foreheads not being mouths. Its surrounding
will look the same up to a flip of what is left or right. With that I mean, if it knew, where it walked off
by knowing that, say, the chin, is below its starting location, it also could know into which horizontal direction it walked off.
If at some point, the ant realizes that the chin is "more" to the right of itself, than it knows, that it has walked off to the right.
Vice versa for walking off left.

This symmetry has a name ($Z_2$ I am guessing, but I cannot remember for sure). Now immagine the ant not walking on a face or butterfly
but on a completely round ball. Now, the ant has no way of telling into which direction it had wandered off. What is more,
it does not even matter where it starts to wander off. In fact, by lack of an _external_ labeling of all the points on the ball
it would not even have a way of telling where it was to begin with. One could remedy this by _embedding_ the ball into a surrounding space, where
every point is labeled (which we could call a coordinate system), but mind that modern differential geometry is not very fond of embedding
manifolds into higher dimensional spaces. Indeed, they seem to make a big deal of not having to embed anything into anything but reminding you
that they could if they wanted to (Whitney).

That being said, return to balls. Physicists and mathematicians love balls, but they seem to like their surface even more. This surface
has its own special mathematical name, $\mathbb{S}^2$. The $2$ stands for it being two dimensional, since it would just be a (two dimensional)
plane once we unroll it. Now, if we get rid of the ball and only consider its surface which we can characterize as all points that have the same
distance from its center (namely the ball's radius) and shift this center into the origin (all zeros) of the coordinate system, because, heck, we
could not care less about an _external_ coordinate system, we can write
$$ \mathbb{S}^2 \equiv \{ (x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R} | \mathsqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} = R \} $$
as we like to use capital letters for constants.

The sphere is a prime example of symmetry. And you might have guessed it, it is rotational symmetry we are speaking about. Turn a ball into any direction you like,
it will still look the same. In fact, you can rotate it into any direction you like, by any **amount** you like, and it will still look the same.
This encapsulates a far deeper topic of infinitesimal symmetries. On the other hand, rotating a square only a tiny amount will result in a different looking
picture. The square does only have discrete rotational symmetries. That is you can only turn it around multiples of 90° ($\pi/2$ as smart people say) for it still
looking the same. Note, that you could also do nothing to the square and it will look the same. Not very profound, but indeed we like our good old _identity_ mapping.
You could also do nothing, then rotate it by a multiple of 90°, and it will also look like you have done nothing. This is not because, rotating a square by 90° and then
doing nothing are inverse to another but more because they are practically the same ("practically the same" normal people say, "isomorphic" mathematicians say).
Speaking of inverses, you could also do nothing and then rotate the square into the opposite direction you did previously. Still, allthesame.
Funny that inverses are somehow practically the same as their inverses.
We now have everything together to study the underlying structure of symmetries: Groups.
